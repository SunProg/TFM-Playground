# Diagnosing Hypothesis Collapse in nanoTabPFN

The cleanest diagnosis is to build synthetic tasks where single-query marginals look identical whether the model preserves hypotheses or collapses them, but the joint predictions differ.

That distinction matters because a deterministic hidden state is not automatically a problem. A deterministic PFN can still correctly encode a Bayesian predictive distribution. You need an experiment where “correct Bayesian averaging” and “mode collapse” make different observable predictions.

nanoTabPFN is suitable because it conditions test predictions on the whole support set using alternating feature and datapoint attention, while each test point attends only to training data.

## 1. Construct two ambiguous task families

Let the latent task be

$$
z \in \{A,B\},
\qquad
P(z=A)=P(z=B)=0.5.
$$

Create two functions that agree on the support region but differ elsewhere. For example:

$$
f_A(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<0\\
0,&x\ge0
\end{cases}
$$

$$
f_B(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<0\\
1,&x\ge0.
\end{cases}
$$

Generate the support examples only from $x<0$:

$$
D=\{(x_i,0):x_i<0\}.
$$

Therefore, the support set cannot distinguish task A from task B:

$$
P(z=A\mid D)=P(z=B\mid D)=0.5.
$$

Now choose two queries in the disagreement region:

$$
x_1^*>0,\qquad x_2^*>0.
$$

Their labels are:

| Latent task | $y_1^*$ | $y_2^*$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 0 | 0 |
| B | 1 | 1 |

The true joint predictive distribution is therefore:

$$
P(0,0\mid D)=0.5,
$$

$$
P(1,1\mid D)=0.5,
$$

and

$$
P(0,1\mid D)=P(1,0\mid D)=0.
$$

The two queries must agree because they come from the same underlying task.

## 2. Why ordinary accuracy cannot diagnose collapse

For either query considered separately:

$$
P(y_j^*=1\mid D)=0.5.
$$

So nanoTabPFN may output:

$$
P(y_1^*=1)=0.5,
\qquad
P(y_2^*=1)=0.5.
$$

These marginal predictions are perfectly Bayesian. But they do not tell us whether the model understands:

> Either both labels are 0 or both labels are 1

rather than:

> Each label is independently random

Both interpretations give marginal probability 0.5. Therefore, metrics such as accuracy, Brier score, calibration, and per-query negative log-likelihood cannot reveal hypothesis collapse in this example.

## 3. Recover the model’s implied joint distribution

Although nanoTabPFN does not directly output a joint distribution over test labels, you can obtain one using the probability chain rule.

First predict $y_1^*$:

$$
\hat p(y_1\mid D,x_1^*).
$$

Then, for each possible value $c\in\{0,1\}$, temporarily add the hypothetical labelled query to the support set:

$$
D_c=D\cup\{(x_1^*,c)\}.
$$

Run nanoTabPFN again to predict $y_2^*$:

$$
\hat p(y_2\mid D_c,x_2^*).
$$

The implied joint distribution is

$$
\hat p(y_1=c,y_2=d\mid D)
=
\hat p(y_1=c\mid D)
\hat p(y_2=d\mid D_c).
$$

This requires:

1. One prediction for $x_1^*$.
2. One conditional prediction for $x_2^*$ assuming $y_1^*=0$.
3. One conditional prediction for $x_2^*$ assuming $y_1^*=1$.

No architecture modification is required.

## 4. What preservation versus collapse would look like

### Correct hypothesis preservation

Initially:

$$
\hat p(y_1=0\mid D)\approx0.5,
\qquad
\hat p(y_1=1\mid D)\approx0.5.
$$

After hypothetically revealing $y_1=0$, the model should infer task A:

$$
\hat p(y_2=0\mid D,y_1=0)\approx1.
$$

After revealing $y_1=1$, it should infer task B:

$$
\hat p(y_2=1\mid D,y_1=1)\approx1.
$$

Thus:

$$
\hat p(0,0)\approx0.5,
\qquad
\hat p(1,1)\approx0.5,
$$

with nearly no probability on $(0,1)$ or $(1,0)$.

### Hypothesis collapse or incoherent averaging

A collapsed model might continue predicting 0.5 after the first label is revealed:

$$
\hat p(y_2=1\mid D,y_1=0)\approx0.5,
$$

$$
\hat p(y_2=1\mid D,y_1=1)\approx0.5.
$$

Its implied joint becomes:

$$
\hat p(0,0)\approx0.25,
\quad
\hat p(0,1)\approx0.25,
$$

$$
\hat p(1,0)\approx0.25,
\quad
\hat p(1,1)\approx0.25.
$$

The marginals are still correct, but half the probability is assigned to impossible cross-mode combinations.

## 5. Primary collapse metric

Define the incoherent probability mass:

$$
C_{\mathrm{incoherent}}
=
\hat p(0,1\mid D)
+
\hat p(1,0\mid D).
$$

For the example:

- Ideal Bayesian predictor: $C_{\mathrm{incoherent}}=0$.
- Independent marginal predictor: $C_{\mathrm{incoherent}}=0.5$.

A normalized score could be:

$$
C_{\mathrm{norm}}
=
\frac{
\sum_{\mathbf y\notin\mathcal Y_{\mathrm{valid}}}
\hat p(\mathbf y\mid D)
}{
1-
\sum_{\mathbf y\notin\mathcal Y_{\mathrm{valid}}}
p_{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\mathbf y\mid D)
}.
$$

Here, $\mathcal Y_{\mathrm{valid}}$ is the set of label vectors generated by at least one valid latent task. In this simple case:

$$
\mathcal Y_{\mathrm{valid}}=\{(0,0),(1,1)\}.
$$

## 6. Compare the complete joint distribution

Because the synthetic generator gives the exact Bayesian joint distribution, calculate:

$$
\operatorname{KL}
\left(
p_{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\mathbf y\mid D)
\;\middle\|\;
\hat p(\mathbf y\mid D)
\right),
$$

or preferably Jensen–Shannon divergence:

$$
\operatorname{JS}
\left(
p_{\mathrm{Bayes}},
\hat p
\right).
$$

Jensen–Shannon divergence is numerically safer when one distribution places zero probability on some outcomes.

You should report both:

- Marginal divergence.
- Joint divergence.

The key signature of hidden hypothesis collapse would be:

$$
\text{low marginal error}
\quad+\quad
\text{high joint error}.
$$

## 7. Test order consistency

The chain-rule joint can also be computed in the reverse order:

$$
\hat p_{1\rightarrow2}(y_1,y_2\mid D)
=
\hat p(y_1\mid D)
\hat p(y_2\mid D,y_1),
$$

and

$$
\hat p_{2\rightarrow1}(y_1,y_2\mid D)
=
\hat p(y_2\mid D)
\hat p(y_1\mid D,y_2).
$$

A coherent probabilistic model should approximately satisfy:

$$
\hat p_{1\rightarrow2}
\approx
\hat p_{2\rightarrow1}.
$$

Measure:

$$
C_{\mathrm{order}}
=
\operatorname{JS}
\left(
\hat p_{1\rightarrow2},
\hat p_{2\rightarrow1}
\right).
$$

Large order dependence indicates that nanoTabPFN’s conditional predictions do not correspond to one consistent joint posterior. This may be an even broader issue than hypothesis collapse.

## 8. Extend beyond two queries

Use $m$ query points that all depend on the same latent task. For example:

$$
\mathbf y_A=(0,0,0,0),
\qquad
\mathbf y_B=(1,1,1,1).
$$

The true posterior is:

$$
p(\mathbf y\mid D)
=
0.5\delta_{\mathbf y_A}
+
0.5\delta_{\mathbf y_B}.
$$

A mode-preserving predictor places mass on only two coherent vectors. An independently averaged predictor distributes mass over all $2^m$ possible vectors.

For $m=4$, only 2 of 16 vectors are valid. This makes collapse much easier to detect.

You can obtain the autoregressive joint:

$$
\hat p(\mathbf y\mid D)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{m}
\hat p
\left(
y_j
\mid
D,\,
(x_1^*,y_1),\ldots,(x_{j-1}^*,y_{j-1}),
x_j^*
\right).
$$

Exact enumeration costs $2^m$ branches for binary classification, so initially use $m=3$ or $4$.

## 9. Use several ambiguity structures

Do not rely on only one toy example. Construct multiple task-mixture priors.

### Global mode ambiguity

Two functions agree over the support region and differ everywhere in the query region.

### Local ambiguity

The functions differ only in a narrow region.

### Feature ambiguity

Task A depends on feature 1:

$$
y=\mathbf 1[x_1>0],
$$

while task B depends on feature 2:

$$
y=\mathbf 1[x_2>0].
$$

Choose support examples where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are correlated, so both rules explain the support labels. Choose query points that break this correlation.

This directly tests whether feature attention preserves competing feature-level explanations.

### Row-subpopulation ambiguity

Task A is explained by one subset of support rows; task B is explained by another. Both fit the observed labels but extrapolate differently.

This targets datapoint attention.

### More than two modes

Use:

$$
z\in\{1,2,3,4\}.
$$

Then test whether the model preserves four coherent label patterns rather than averaging them independently.

## 10. Vary the amount of ambiguity

Create a continuum from fully ambiguous to fully identifiable. Let $r$ be the number of disambiguating support examples.

- $r=0$: posterior over tasks is approximately uniform.
- Small $r$: posterior is multimodal but unequal.
- Large $r$: posterior concentrates on one task.

Because the synthetic generator is known, calculate $p(z\mid D)$ exactly or by enumeration. Then test whether nanoTabPFN’s implied joint distribution evolves correctly as $r$ increases.

Expected behaviour:

> No disambiguating examples → multiple coherent modes  
> A few disambiguating examples → one mode gains weight  
> Many disambiguating examples → posterior collapses correctly to one task

The important distinction is between:

- Desirable posterior concentration due to evidence.
- Undesirable hypothesis collapse despite unresolved ambiguity.

## 11. Inspect hidden representations as supporting evidence

The predictive joint test should be primary. Representation analysis is secondary.

You could extract:

- Test-target embeddings.
- Pooled support embeddings.
- Attention matrices across layers.

Then train lightweight probes to predict $p(z\mid D)$ or properties such as posterior entropy $H[z\mid D]$.

However, be careful: one deterministic vector can encode an entire posterior distribution. Failure to form visibly separated clusters does not prove collapse.

More useful representation tests include:

### Posterior decoding

Can a linear or shallow probe recover the true vector

$$
[p(z=1\mid D),\ldots,p(z=K\mid D)]?
$$

### Ambiguity decoding

Can the representation predict $H(z\mid D)$?

### Conditional update geometry

After adding one disambiguating labelled query, does the hidden representation move toward the correct task-specific region?

These analyses help explain the mechanism but should not replace the output-level joint test.

## 12. Essential baselines

Compare nanoTabPFN against:

- The exact Bayes predictor under the synthetic prior.
- An independent-marginal oracle.
- k-nearest neighbours.
- Random forest.
- A transformer explicitly trained to output task identity.
- A mixture model with an explicit latent task variable.
- An ensemble of nanoTabPFNs, if computationally feasible.

The especially important baseline is the independent-marginal oracle. It has perfect per-query calibration but no joint coherence. Your proposed diagnostic must clearly distinguish it from the true Bayesian predictor.

## The strongest experiment

The central result should look like this:

| Model | Marginal NLL ↓ | Joint NLL ↓ | Incoherent mass ↓ | Order inconsistency ↓ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Exact Bayes | best | best | 0 | 0 |
| Independent oracle | best | poor | high | 0 |
| nanoTabPFN | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| Multi-path PFN | ? | ? | ? | ? |

The decisive finding would be:

> nanoTabPFN matches the Bayes-optimal marginal probabilities but assigns substantial probability to label combinations that no plausible latent task can generate.

That would be concrete evidence that it performs predictive averaging without maintaining coherent task hypotheses. Only then would a multi-trajectory or latent-task PFN have a well-motivated problem to solve.
